Research planning

Designing research questions that survive peer review

Start with the decision your study could inform. Name the population, setting and outcome in a single sentence. Then compare that sentence with what your study can actually observe.

For example, a clinic register may record who returns for follow-up without recording why people miss visits. The register can help you describe attendance. Understanding the reasons may call for interviews and a separate consent process. Resolve that difference before you finalise the question.

Academic writing

How to storyboard your thesis chapters

Write one sentence for each chapter explaining what the reader should know by its end. Read the sentences in order. The gap in the literature should lead to your research question, the question to your methods, and the results to your discussion.

Try this with a supervisor before drafting. If a results chapter promises to explain a change that the methods can only describe, revise the sentence while the outline is still easy to move.

Publication

Choosing the right journal scope

Summarise your manuscript in two sentences. Say who the findings concern and why those readers might use them. Compare that summary with the journal’s aims and the types of articles it accepts.

Read a few recent papers with a similar study design. Check how the authors frame their contribution and whether your article can meet the journal’s word limit, reporting requirements and table allowance.

Research ethics

Responsible use of similarity reports

Read each highlighted passage alongside its matched source. References, quotations, standard terminology and unattributed wording can all contribute to the same overall percentage.

Check that direct quotations are marked and cited. Where you have followed another author’s wording too closely, restate the reasoning in your own words and cite the original work. Follow your institution’s guidance when interpreting the report.

Data analysis

Making statistical choices transparent

Keep a decision log as you work. For each outcome, record how you defined it, which observations you included, how you handled missing values and why the analysis fits the research question.

If you revise a decision after seeing the data, note what changed and why. A colleague should be able to trace the numbers in a results table back to the definitions, data and analysis that produced them.

Communication

Presenting risk and uncertainty

Give readers the event counts, denominators and time period before interpreting a difference. For example, in a hypothetical study, 4 events among 100 participants compared with 2 among 100 is an absolute difference of 2 percentage points.

Present the effect estimate with its confidence interval and explain what the range means for the research question. Keep limitations such as small samples and incomplete follow-up close to the claim they affect.
